That’s all it takes. You were one keystroke away from handing a stranger your student account.

Relax. Those boxes were never real fields, and nothing you did was recorded. But the email you just trusted had six warning signs in it. Here they are.

Action required: Fall disbursement on hold

FACPU Financial Aid Office
finaid@cardinalplains-edu.com1

Hi Student,2

Your Fall 2026 financial aid disbursement has been placed on hold due to an incomplete identity verification.

To avoid cancellation of your refund,3 please verify your NetID within 24 hours.4

[Verify my account]5 and sign in on a page that isn’t your school’s6

  1. A lookalike address. The real school domain is cardinalplains.edu. This one is cardinalplains-edu.com. One hyphen and a “.com” do all the work.
  2. No name. Your financial aid office knows exactly who you are. “Hi Student” means the sender doesn’t.
  3. A threat. Fear of losing money shuts down careful thinking. That’s the point.
  4. A countdown. A 24-hour deadline is designed to make you act before you check.
  5. A button instead of directions. Hovering showed it led off campus. Real offices tell you to log in the way you normally do.
  6. A padlock that means nothing. The fake login had a lock icon too. HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest.

Closer to home

In October 2024, Iowa State investigated a phishing attack in which some people entered their ISU login on a fake page. The bait email was titled “Email System Maintenance,” and it came from a real iastate.edu account the attackers had already taken over. ISU ITS

And it doesn’t stop at one inbox. Stolen student accounts are used to email the rest of campus — fake job offers, or links to download “remote support” software — so each compromised account becomes bait for the next. Iowa State Daily

From the attacker’s side of the screen

What happens in the next 30 minutes.

Say you hadn’t stopped. The second you hit “Sign in,” your password doesn’t go to one place. It’s relayed across the world in seconds, so no single server points back to the person who took it.

tracing credential relay…

  1. Ames, Iowa — youYou submit your NetID and password on the fake page.
  2. Amsterdam — proxyThe data is bounced through a rented server to hide its origin.
  3. Lagos — relayPassed again through a compromised host in another country.
  4. Moscow — collectorYour login lands in a database with thousands of others.
  5. The attacker — anywhereThey buy or use it. You never find out where it went.

Then the next 30 minutes.

session log
  1. 9:02 PM

    You type your NetID and password into the fake page. The attacker’s server saves them instantly. The page then redirects you to the real portal, so nothing seems off.

  2. 9:03 PM

    They sign in as you. Your phone buzzes with an approval request. You literally just logged in, so you tap Approve.

  3. 9:05 PM

    They create an inbox rule: any email containing aid, bank, or password skips your inbox and goes to a folder you never open. You’ll never see the warnings.

  4. 9:09 PM

    They open your student portal and change where your refund gets deposited.

  5. 9:14 PM

    Your account emails a huge list of students a link to “install the new remote-support app.” It comes from a real campus address, so your classmates trust it and click.

  6. 9:31 PM

    You go to bed. Nothing looks wrong.

  7. Tomorrow

    A handful of the students who clicked are now compromised too — and their accounts start sending the same link. The chain keeps going without you.

One click doesn’t stay one click

How a single account takes a campus down with it.

This is the part people miss. The danger isn’t only your account — it’s that your account becomes the next attacker. At a school the size of Iowa State, a few accounts get taken over on a routine basis. Each one is used to email long lists of students a link to download “remote support” or “security” software. A fraction of those students install it or hand over their login, and their accounts send it onward. The chain never fully stops.

It’s not just email anymore

The hook comes in three new shapes.

+1 (689) 204-5531Text Message · Today 2:14 PM
ParcelPost: Your package is being held at our facility due to an incomplete address. Update within 12 hrs to avoid return to sender:

Smishing: phishing by text.

Texts feel personal, links are shortened or hidden, and your phone screen is too small to show the full address. That’s why they work.

Tap the link in the text to see what’s wrong with it.

Your phone is quiet. For now.

MFA fatigue: they already have your password.

Multi-factor authentication stops most stolen-password logins. So attackers spam approval requests, sometimes at 2 a.m., until someone taps Approve just to make it stop.

Iowa State’s own guidance: never share your MFA code with anyone, even someone claiming to be from ISU. ISU ITS

Vishing: phishing by voice.

“Hi, this is Mark from the Cardinal Plains IT Security Office. We detected a login to your student account from overseas and we’ve locked it for your protection. To unlock it, I’ll text you a six-digit code. Just read it back to me when I call in five minutes. Thanks.”

The tell: that “code” is the one-time login code for your account. Mark is signing in as you right now. No real IT office will ever ask you to read a code back.

Voice scams are surging, and AI now clones voices from seconds of audio. The FBI received nearly 61,000 complaints about criminals impersonating law enforcement and government officials between January 2025 and July 2026, with losses over $1.6 billion. FBI IC3

The fix takes ten seconds

When a message pushes you to hurry, go

SLOW

Every phish in this page relied on speed. Taking it away is the whole defense. Check off each step to commit to it.

  1. S

    Stop.

    Urgency is the attack. A deadline, a threat, or a too-good offer is your cue to pause, not to act.

  2. L

    Look.

    Check the sender’s full address, not just the name. Hover over links, or press and hold on mobile, to see where they really go.

  3. O

    Open it yourself.

    Don’t use the link. Type the real address or open the official app. If it’s real, it will be there.

  4. W

    Warn.

    Report it. One report lets your IT team pull the email from everyone else’s inbox.

How to report phishing at Iowa State

  1. In Outlook, select the message.
  2. Choose Report (web) or Report Message (desktop).
  3. Pick Report Phishing.

ITS prefers this method because it receives a full copy of the message. ISU ITS
